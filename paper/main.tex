\begin{abstract}
EquiformerV2 and other recent SO(3)-equivariant networks for atomistic
modeling apply pointwise nonlinearities by mapping spherical-harmonic (SH)
coefficients to a discrete grid on the sphere, applying a scalar activation,
and integrating back via quadrature. The standard implementation, inherited
from \texttt{e3nn}, uses an equiangular Driscoll--Healy (DH) grid. We observe
that DH is the wrong default for this use case: it doubles the latitude
points required for exact integration of band-limited products relative to
Gauss--Legendre (GL) quadrature, yet EquiformerV2 cannot exploit DH's only
algorithmic advantage (FFT-based transforms) because it stores
to/from-grid as dense matrices. The result is an avoidable equivariance
penalty.
We implement a drop-in \texttt{CustomSO3Grid} that replaces DH with GL.
On the fairchem default EquiformerV2 architecture
(12 layers / 128 channels / $\ell_{\max}{=}6$), GL reaches the same
kernel-level S$^2$-activation equivariance error as DH oversampled
$2\times$ at $\mathbf{113.17 \pm 2.25}$~ms per forward---versus
$164.66 \pm 1.62$~ms for DH $2\times$---a savings of $\mathbf{51.5 \pm 2.36}$
\textbf{ms} ($\mathbf{31.3\%}$, Welch's $t$-test $p < 10^{-4}$ on $n{=}5$
run means with distinct QM9 batches per iteration). The wall-clock savings
come entirely from the S2-activation kernel, which grows from $8.5\%$ to
$39.1\%$ of forward time when DH is oversampled, while everything else
(attention, FFN, normalization) is unchanged.
We further verify on five 50-epoch DH-trained QM9 checkpoints that swapping
in GL at inference, without any retraining, preserves predictions: test
MAE shifts by $\leq 0.01$~eV (well within seed-to-seed training variance);
trained-model prediction-invariance error is unchanged (the model has
learned to compensate for its training-time grid, so the
\emph{kernel-level} equivariance gain only translates to
\emph{trained-model} equivariance gain when GL is used at training time
too). The headline operational claim is therefore:
\emph{GL is a drop-in replacement that delivers the wall-clock and
kernel-equivariance benefits with no retraining or accuracy regression.}
We position this against concurrent work that addresses the same equivariance
problem via architectural change (EquiformerV3's SwiGLU-S$^2$,
\texttt{arXiv:2604.09130}) or alternative sampling layouts (Equivariant
Spherical Transformer, \texttt{arXiv:2505.23086}); GL is the textbook
quadrature-side answer and is complementary to both.
\end{abstract}

%========================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%========================================================================

\paragraph{Spherical-harmonic neural networks for atomistic modeling.}
SO(3)-equivariant graph networks have become the standard tool for
molecular and materials property prediction. The line of work from
SCN~\cite{scn} to eSCN~\cite{escn} to EquiformerV2~\cite{equiformerv2}
represents per-node features as functions on the sphere, expanded in
spherical harmonics (SH) up to a band limit $\ell_{\max}$. The dominant
nonlinearity in this design is the \emph{S2 activation}: project SH
coefficients onto a finite grid on $S^2$, apply a scalar activation
pointwise, integrate back via quadrature. This operator is approximately
equivariant in the continuous limit, exactly equivariant only when the
quadrature is exact for the products of band-limited functions involved.

\paragraph{The known equivariance gap and the community response.}
The original EquiformerV2 paper acknowledges that
``sampling-induced equivariance error remains an open
challenge''~\cite{equiformerv2}. The \texttt{fairchem} community has
filed and discussed this issue (\texttt{FAIR-Chem/fairchem\#1068},
March 2025), where users observe that equivariance error of order
$\sim 10^{-2}$ is reached at the default grid, dropping only to
$\sim 10^{-4}$ at $1024^2$ grid points~\cite{fairchem1068}---a
prohibitive cost. Two very recent papers attack the same problem with
different tools. \emph{EquiformerV3}~\cite{equiformerv3}
(arXiv 2604.09130, April 2026, by the V2 authors) replaces the S$^2$
activation with a SwiGLU-style activation that operates only on
scalars and bilinear products, achieving a $50.6\%$ reduction in
grid points while maintaining strict equivariance, but as an
\emph{architectural} change requiring retraining.
\emph{Equivariant Spherical Transformer (EST)}~\cite{est} (arXiv
2505.23086, May 2025) replaces the e3nn grid with a Fibonacci
lattice, a heuristically more uniform sampling pattern, observing
better empirical expressiveness.

\paragraph{The choice nobody has made: a different quadrature.}
What none of these works alters is the choice of \emph{quadrature
rule}. EquiformerV2's \texttt{SO3\_Grid} delegates to e3nn's
\texttt{ToS2Grid} / \texttt{FromS2Grid}, which use an equiangular
Driscoll--Healy (DH) grid with Kostelec--Rockmore weights. This was a
sensible inheritance: DH supports fast O($N \log N$) FFT-based forward and
inverse SH transforms, which is the algorithmic property e3nn was built
around for fast spherical convolutions. But EquiformerV2 does not use
the FFT---it precomputes \texttt{to\_grid\_mat} and
\texttt{from\_grid\_mat} as dense matrices and applies them via
\texttt{einsum}. Without the FFT, DH's advantage evaporates and its
disadvantage---needing $\sim 2\times$ as many latitude nodes as
Gauss--Legendre (GL) for exact integration of degree-$2\ell_{\max}$
products---becomes pure overhead. The classical numerical-analysis
result that \emph{GL needs roughly half the latitude points of DH for
matched accuracy on the sphere}~\cite{shtools,glq_dh_planet} is more
than a century old, and is the standard textbook fact about spherical
quadrature. It does not appear to have been applied to the
EquiformerV2 line of work.

\paragraph{This paper.}
We make this swap and measure what happens at three levels.
(i)~\emph{Kernel level}: equivariance error of a single $S^2$-activation
under each grid (Sec.~``Kernel-level equivariance'', weight-independent;
GL match-DH and DH $2\times$ both reach the truncation floor while DH
default and GL min stay above it).
(ii)~\emph{Architecture level}: end-to-end forward wall-clock under each
grid using a freshly-instantiated 12-layer EquiformerV2 backbone, with
all measurements on \emph{independently-sampled} QM9 batches (no batch
reuse within a run) and all confidence intervals computed over $n{=}5$
run means with a $t$-distribution at $df{=}4$. This is what a deployed
model would cost; the cost depends only on architecture and tensor
shapes, not on weight values, so loading checkpoints is unnecessary
here.
(iii)~\emph{Drop-in on trained checkpoints}: load five actual 50-epoch
QM9 checkpoints trained with DH default, swap in GL without any
retraining or fine-tuning, and re-measure prediction invariance error
and test MAE on the held-out test set. We find that test predictions
are essentially unchanged ($|\Delta \text{MAE}| \leq 0.01$~eV on a
$\sim 4$~eV scale, well within training variance), while trained-model
prediction-invariance error is also unchanged. The interpretation is
that the trained network has internalized whatever grid it was trained
with; replacing the grid post-hoc gives the wall-clock benefit without
hurting accuracy, but extracting kernel-level equivariance gains in the
trained model also requires training with GL. We discuss this
distinction in Sec.~``Drop-in vs.\ retraining''.

The result is a clean Pareto improvement (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}):
GL with $14 \times 13$ latitude $\times$ longitude nodes ($182$ points)
reaches the same equivariance error as the DH $2\times$ oversampling
($28 \times 28 = 784$ points), at $113.17 \pm 2.25$~ms vs.\
$164.66 \pm 1.62$~ms per forward, a saving of
$\mathbf{31.3\%}$ ($p < 10^{-4}$). GL with the same latitude budget as
DH default ($14 \times 5 = 70$ points) costs the same wall-clock as DH
default ($\Delta = -0.33 \pm 2.49$~ms, $p = 0.76$, n.s.) but achieves
substantially better kernel-level equivariance.

\paragraph{Why the savings are so big.}
We hook CUDA timing events into every \texttt{S2Activation.forward}
inside the model and find that the S2 activation alone
accounts for $8.5\%$ of forward time at DH default, but balloons to
$39.1\%$ at DH $2\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:breakdown}). The remaining
$\sim 100$~ms (attention, FFN, edge embeddings, normalization, SO(2)
convolutions) is constant across configurations to within $1.5\%$
relative variance. So although the S2 activation does not
\emph{dominate} forward time in absolute terms at the default grid, it
fully \emph{dominates the delta} between configurations: $100\%$ of the
wall-clock gap between DH default and DH $2\times$ is the S2 activation
kernel. GL dodges this gap entirely because at the model's actual
workload, the einsum kernels are bottlenecked by launch and
tensor-allocation overhead rather than by FLOPs, so increasing the GL
grid up to $2\times$ is essentially free. The breakdown table below
uses CUDA-event timing inside the same architecture-level (random-init)
forward used for the Pareto wall-clock; the per-call values are
weight-independent properties of the kernel.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We identify that EquiformerV2 inherits e3nn's Driscoll--Healy
    quadrature without inheriting its only algorithmic advantage (FFT-based
    transforms), making the inheritance pure overhead in this setting.
  \item We build a drop-in \texttt{CustomSO3Grid} module supporting
    Gauss--Legendre quadrature that swaps the to-grid and from-grid
    matrices in any \texttt{EquiformerV2Backbone}, including pretrained
    checkpoints. No retraining or architectural change is required.
  \item On the fairchem default EquiformerV2
    (12 layers / 128 channels / $\ell_{\max}{=}6$), we measure end-to-end
    forward wall-clock under a rigorous protocol (5 independent runs,
    each on 30 distinct QM9 batches, no batch reuse) and find that
    GL match-DH reaches the same kernel-level $S^2$-activation
    equivariance as DH $2\times$ oversampling at
    $\mathbf{31.3\%}$ \textbf{lower wall-clock}
    ($113.17 \pm 2.25$ vs.\ $164.66 \pm 1.62$~ms per forward;
    Welch's $t$-test on $n{=}5$ run means, $p < 10^{-4}$). All GL
    configurations are statistically indistinguishable from DH default
    in wall-clock ($p > 0.4$).
  \item A per-operation CUDA-event breakdown shows that the
    $S^2$-activation kernel accounts for $100\%$ of the wall-clock gap
    between configurations (the non-S$^2$ portion of forward varies by
    only $1.5\%$ across all grids). At DH $2\times$ the kernel's share
    of forward time grows from $8.5\%$ to $39.1\%$.
  \item We validate the swap's safety on five 50-epoch DH-trained QM9
    checkpoints: changing to GL at inference shifts test MAE by
    $\leq 0.01$~eV (within seed variance) and leaves prediction-invariance
    error unchanged. The trained model has internalized its training-time
    grid; replacing the grid post-hoc inherits the wall-clock benefit
    without harming accuracy, but realising kernel-level equivariance
    gains in the trained model also requires training with GL.
  \item We position the result against concurrent architectural fixes
    (EquiformerV3's SwiGLU-S$^2$, EST's Fibonacci lattice). GL operates
    on a different lever (the quadrature rule, not the activation or the
    sampling pattern) and is therefore complementary; in particular,
    it can be applied to existing pretrained checkpoints today.
\end{enumerate}
