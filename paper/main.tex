\title{Beyond Driscoll--Healy: Drop-in Quadrature Upgrades for EquiformerV2's
$S^2$ Activation}

\author{%
  Anonymous Authors\\
}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
EquiformerV2 and other recent SO(3)-equivariant atomistic networks
implement a pointwise $S^2$ activation by mapping spherical-harmonic
coefficients to a discrete grid, applying a scalar nonlinearity, and
integrating back. The default grid, inherited from \texttt{e3nn},
uses Driscoll--Healy (DH) equiangular quadrature. We argue that this
default is not the best choice for EquiformerV2, and that the choice
of \emph{quadrature method} (rather than the activation function or
the sampling pattern, the targets of concurrent work) is itself a
useful lever for the speed/accuracy trade-off. We instantiate two
alternative quadratures---Gauss--Legendre (GL) and Lebedev---as
drop-in replacements for the default grid.
On the fairchem-default 12-layer EquiformerV2 ($\ell_{\max}{=}6$),
GL match-DH reaches the same $S^2$-activation equivariance error as
the standard DH-$2\times$ oversampling at $113.17 \pm 2.25$ ms per
forward (versus $164.66 \pm 1.62$ ms for DH-$2\times$): a saving of
$51.5 \pm 2.36$ ms ($31.3\%$, $p < 10^{-4}$, Welch's $t$-test on
$n{=}5$ run means with distinct QM9 batches per iteration). At the
kernel level Lebedev (degree~21, 170 points) is in fact Pareto-better
than GL by an additional $\sim$28\% though it does not fit
EquiformerV2's tensor-product einsum signature without minor
adaptation.
The swap is safe in inference: on five 50-epoch DH-trained QM9
checkpoints the test MAE shifts by $\leq 0.01$~eV and the per-layer
$\ell{=}0$ invariance profile is preserved across all grids. We use
this preservation to give a mechanistic account of the per-layer
error dynamics: a universal $1.5$--$16\times$ jump from layer~0 to
layer~1 followed by a plateau, set by the cross-degree coupling
factor of the SO(2) convolution and the smoothness of the activation,
with the absolute floor fixed by the training-time grid.
\end{abstract}

%========================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%========================================================================

The recent line of SO(3)-equivariant graph networks for molecular and
materials property prediction~\cite{scn,escn,equiformerv2} represents
per-node features as functions on the sphere, expanded in spherical
harmonics (SH) up to a band limit $\ell_{\max}$. The dominant
nonlinearity in this design is the \emph{$S^2$ activation}: it
projects SH coefficients onto a finite grid on $S^2$, applies a scalar
activation pointwise, and integrates back via quadrature. This operator
is exactly equivariant only when the quadrature integrates the relevant
products of band-limited functions exactly; otherwise its equivariance
violation propagates through the rest of the network.

The size of this violation is well known to the community. The
EquiformerV2 paper itself notes that ``sampling-induced equivariance
error remains an open challenge''~\cite{equiformerv2}, and the fairchem
issue tracker (\texttt{FAIR-Chem/fairchem\#1068}, March~2025) records
users observing equivariance error of order $\sim 10^{-2}$ at the
default grid and only $\sim 10^{-4}$ at $1024^2$ grid points---a
prohibitive cost~\cite{fairchem1068}. Two recent papers attack the
problem with different tools. EquiformerV3~\cite{equiformerv3}
replaces the $S^2$ activation with a SwiGLU-style operator on scalars
and bilinear products, achieving a $50\%$ reduction in grid points but
requiring an architectural change and retraining. The Equivariant
Spherical Transformer (EST)~\cite{est} replaces the e3nn grid with a
Fibonacci lattice---a heuristically more uniform sampling pattern.
What none of these works changes is the \emph{quadrature rule}.

EquiformerV2's \texttt{SO3\_Grid} delegates to e3nn's
\texttt{ToS2Grid}/\texttt{FromS2Grid}, which use an equiangular
Driscoll--Healy (DH) grid with Kostelec--Rockmore weights. This was a
defensible inheritance because DH supports an FFT-based fast SH
transform, the algorithmic property around which e3nn was originally
designed. EquiformerV2, however, does not use the FFT: its
\texttt{SO3\_Grid} precomputes \texttt{to\_grid\_mat} and
\texttt{from\_grid\_mat} as dense matrices and applies them through
\texttt{einsum}~(\S\ref{sec:bg}). Once committed to dense matrix
multiplication, the question collapses to ``which set of nodes
integrates band-limited products with the fewest points?'' and the
textbook answer is no longer Driscoll--Healy: Gauss--Legendre needs
roughly half the latitude points of DH for matched
exactness~\cite{shtools,glq_dh_planet,driscoll_healy}, and Lebedev
rules can do better still for moderate-degree spherical integrands.

In this paper we treat the choice of quadrature method as an explicit
hyperparameter of the $S^2$ activation. We expose it through a drop-in
\texttt{CustomSO3Grid} module that replaces the default DH grid in
any EquiformerV2 backbone~(\S\ref{sec:method}). We instantiate two
alternatives, Gauss--Legendre and Lebedev, and characterise their
behaviour at three levels: equivariance error of the bare
$S^2$-Activation kernel, end-to-end forward wall-clock under a
rigorous independent-replicate protocol~(\S\ref{sec:headline}), and
preservation of trained-model predictions when the grid is swapped
post-hoc into existing checkpoints~(\S\ref{sec:dropin}). Per-layer
measurements of the $\ell{=}0$ invariance error connect the two,
exposing a universal two-phase pattern---a sharp jump from layer~0
to layer~1, then a plateau---whose absolute floor is set by the
training-time grid.

We make four contributions. \emph{First}, we identify the choice of
quadrature method as a separate axis of speed/accuracy improvement
for EquiformerV2's $S^2$ activation, complementary to the
architectural and sampling-pattern fixes proposed concurrently
in~\cite{equiformerv3} and~\cite{est}, and demonstrate it with two
concrete instantiations (Gauss--Legendre and Lebedev) deployed
through a drop-in module. \emph{Second}, on the fairchem-default
12-layer EquiformerV2 we measure that GL match-DH reaches the same
equivariance floor as the standard DH $2\times$ oversampling at
$31.3\%$ lower wall-clock per forward
($113.17 \pm 2.25$ vs.~$164.66 \pm 1.62$~ms, $p < 10^{-4}$ on $n{=}5$
independent replicates), while the GL configurations themselves are
statistically indistinguishable from the DH default in wall-clock
($p > 0.4$). \emph{Third}, on five 50-epoch DH-trained QM9
checkpoints, swapping the grid at inference shifts test MAE by at
most $0.01$~eV and leaves the trained-model prediction-invariance
ratio at $1.00 \pm 0.02$, establishing the swap as a deployment-ready
drop-in for inference cost. \emph{Fourth}, a per-layer analysis
exposes a universal $1.5$--$16\times$ jump at layer~0$\to$1 followed
by a plateau, captured by a $\delta$/$c$ toy model in which $\delta$
is the per-$S^2$-Act $\ell{>}0$ perturbation and $c$ is the
cross-degree coupling factor. The shape is set by the architecture;
the \emph{absolute floor} is set by the training-time grid's
\emph{point count}, not its method---training with GL match-DH (182
points) gives a profile statistically indistinguishable from DH
default (70 points), while training with the much denser DH~$2\times$
(784 points) lowers the floor by $\sim 160\times$. This explains why
an inference-time swap preserves predictions (the profile does not
move under it) and why a trained-model equivariance gain requires
training with substantially more grid points than the e3nn default;
GL gives the same wall-clock as DH default at any matched grid
budget, so a denser GL grid (e.g.\ GL $2\times$) is the natural
intervention.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:bg}
%========================================================================

A per-node feature in an SO(3)-equivariant network on $S^2$ is a
vector $c \in \mathbb{R}^{(\ell_{\max}+1)^2}$ of spherical-harmonic
coefficients indexed by $(\ell, m)$ with $0 \leq \ell \leq \ell_{\max}$
and $-\ell \leq m \leq \ell$. EquiformerV2 additionally crops to
$|m| \leq m_{\max} \leq \ell_{\max}$, an SO(3)-to-SO(2) reduction
introduced by eSCN~\cite{escn}. Given any quadrature
$\{(\theta_i, \phi_i, w_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ on the sphere with
$\sum_i w_i = 4\pi$ and a scalar function
$\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the $S^2$ activation
$\mathcal{A}_\sigma$ is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}_\sigma(c)_{\ell m}
  \;=\;
  \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \,
  \sigma\!\left(\,\sum_{\ell',m'} c_{\ell' m'}\,
        Y_{\ell' m'}(\theta_i, \phi_i)\right)
  Y_{\ell m}(\theta_i, \phi_i),
  \label{eq:s2act}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{\ell m}$ are the real spherical harmonics. Equation
\eqref{eq:s2act} factorises naturally as two linear maps separated by
a pointwise nonlinearity: a forward projection $c \mapsto f$ that
evaluates $f(\theta_i, \phi_i) = \sum_{\ell', m'} c_{\ell' m'}
Y_{\ell' m'}(\theta_i, \phi_i)$ at every grid point, the application
of $\sigma$ to each $f(\theta_i, \phi_i)$, and a backward
projection that integrates against $w_i Y_{\ell m}(\theta_i, \phi_i)$.
EquiformerV2's \texttt{S2Activation.forward} implements exactly this
factorisation (Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}, top),
with the two linear maps stored as dense buffers
$\texttt{to\_grid\_mat}_{i, (\ell m)} = Y_{\ell m}(\theta_i, \phi_i)$
and
$\texttt{from\_grid\_mat}_{i, (\ell m)} = w_i \,
Y_{\ell m}(\theta_i, \phi_i)$. Because the buffers are precomputed
once at model construction, replacing the grid amounts to replacing
these two buffers; no other code in the network changes.

The first three stages of \eqref{eq:s2act} (expansion in spherical
harmonics, evaluation at grid points, pointwise $\sigma$) are exact,
continuous operations on the underlying band-limited function
$f(\theta, \phi)$ and commute with rotations of $f$. The
\emph{only} step that introduces equivariance error is the final
integration: the exact integral
$c'_{\ell m} = \int_{S^2} \sigma(f(\theta,\phi))\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta,
\phi)\,d\Omega$ is replaced by the finite-quadrature sum in
\eqref{eq:s2act}, and the gap between them is what breaks
equivariance. The intuition is spectral
(Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}, bottom): although $f$
is band-limited at $\ell_{\max}$, the pointwise nonlinearity $\sigma$
creates content at arbitrarily high degrees in $\sigma(f)$. An
$N$-point grid can only faithfully integrate spherical polynomials up
to a band limit set by $N$; high-$\ell$ content of $\sigma(f)$
aliases back into the kept $\ell \leq \ell_{\max}$ coefficients, and
the aliased contribution depends on the orientation of the grid
relative to $f$. Section~\ref{sec:method} chooses a quadrature whose
band limit aligns with $\ell_{\max}$, and
Appendix~\ref{app:exact-equivariance} formalises the limit:
the operator obtained by replacing the quadrature sum with the exact
integral is exactly equivariant for any $\sigma$.

We will distinguish two equivariance metrics throughout. The first is
the \emph{kernel-level} equivariance error of a single $S^2$
activation, which we define as the expected relative residual under
random rotations and random SH coefficients. Let $\mathcal{D}(R)$
denote the Wigner-$D$ matrix associated with a rotation
$R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ (acting on the SH-coefficient vector by
$c \mapsto \mathcal{D}(R) c$):
\begin{equation}
  e_{\rm kernel}(\mathcal{A}_\sigma)
  \;=\;
  \mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\substack{c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \\
                                 R \sim \mathrm{Haar}(\mathrm{SO}(3))}}
  \!\left[\,
  \frac{\bigl\| \mathcal{A}_\sigma\!\bigl(\mathcal{D}(R)\,c\bigr)
              \;-\; \mathcal{D}(R)\, \mathcal{A}_\sigma(c) \bigr\|}
       {\bigl\| \mathcal{A}_\sigma\!\bigl(\mathcal{D}(R)\,c\bigr) \bigr\|}
  \,\right].
  \label{eq:kerneleq}
\end{equation}
In practice we estimate \eqref{eq:kerneleq} as a Monte Carlo average
over 50 independent $(c, R)$ pairs (5 rotations $\times$ 10 random
inputs in the experiments below). This metric depends only on the
grid and the activation $\sigma$; it does not require a trained
model. The second is the \emph{trained-model prediction invariance}
of the full network's output $y$ on a molecular input $x$:
\begin{equation}
  e_{\rm pred}
  \;=\;
  \mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\substack{x \sim \mathcal{X}_{\rm test} \\
                                 R \sim \mathrm{Haar}(\mathrm{SO}(3))}}
  \!\left[\,
  \frac{\bigl| y(x) - y(R \cdot x) \bigr|}
       {\bigl| y(x) \bigr|}
  \,\right],
  \label{eq:predeq}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}_{\rm test}$ is the held-out test set and
$R \cdot x$ denotes the rotation of atom positions by $R$. This
metric depends on the trained weights, the architecture, and
\emph{every} $S^2$ activation in the network; we estimate it as an
average over 10 rotations $\times$ 20 test batches. The two metrics
agree qualitatively but differ by orders of magnitude in absolute
scale ($e_{\rm kernel} \sim 10^{-1}$ vs.\ $e_{\rm pred} \sim 10^{-5}$
in our setting); we always state explicitly which one each number
reports.

The choice of quadrature in \eqref{eq:s2act} amounts to choosing
$\{(\theta_i, \phi_i, w_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, and is the central design
question this paper addresses (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\section{Method: \texttt{CustomSO3Grid}}
\label{sec:method}
%========================================================================

We consider three quadrature families on $S^2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:quadratures}).
\emph{Driscoll--Healy} uses an equiangular tensor-product grid
$\theta_j = (j+\tfrac{1}{2})\pi/N_\theta$, $\phi_k = 2\pi k/N_\phi$,
with $N_\theta = 2(\ell_{\max}+1)$ latitude points and
$N_\phi \geq 2\ell_{\max}+1$ longitude points~\cite{driscoll_healy}.
\emph{Gauss--Legendre} places $\theta_j$ at the roots of the Legendre
polynomial $P_{N_\theta}(\cos\theta_j) = 0$ with companion weights,
which is exact for band-limited products of degree $\leq 2\ell_{\max}$
using only $N_\theta = \ell_{\max}+1$ latitude points---a factor of
two fewer than DH~\cite{shtools}; $\phi$ is again uniform.
\emph{Lebedev} rules~\cite{lebedev_quadrature} give a non-tensor-product
set of $N$ nodes on the sphere designed to be exact for all spherical
polynomials up to a given degree with as few points as possible:
degree~21 with 170 points is the smallest rule exact at
$\ell_{\max}{=}6$, far below DH's $14 \times 13 = 182$ points or
DH~$2\times$'s $28 \times 28 = 784$ points.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig2/fig_quadratures.pdf}
  \caption{Three quadrature node layouts on $S^2$ for
    $\ell_{\max}=4$. Driscoll--Healy (left) uses an equiangular
    tensor-product grid with $2(\ell_{\max}+1)$ latitude rings, twice
    what Gauss--Legendre (middle) needs for the same exactness.
    Lebedev (right) avoids the tensor-product structure entirely,
    distributing roughly the same number of points more uniformly
    over the sphere. All three integrate spherical polynomials of
    degree $\leq 2\ell_{\max}$ exactly. Replacing one for another

    \texttt{from\_grid\_mat}; the rest of the

  \label{fig:quadratures}
\end{figure}

We expose all three through a drop-in module \texttt{CustomSO3Grid}
that replaces fairchem's \texttt{SO3\_Grid}. It uses the same
\texttt{get\_to\_grid\_mat} / \texttt{get\_from\_grid\_mat}
interface, with matrices of identical shape signature; a
\texttt{patch\_so3\_grid()} helper swaps every grid in
\texttt{model.backbone.SO3\_grid} after model construction, so
existing pretrained weights remain valid.

\subsection{Constructing the to/from-grid matrices}

For the tensor-product cases (DH and GL) the grid factorises as
$\{(\theta_j, \phi_k, w_j^{(\theta)} w_k^{(\phi)})\}$ with $1 \leq j
\leq N_\theta$ latitude points and $1 \leq k \leq N_\phi$ longitude
points. The longitude weights are uniform,
$w_k^{(\phi)} = 2\pi/N_\phi$. The latitude nodes and weights differ:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \emph{Driscoll--Healy:}
  $\theta_j^{\rm DH} = (j-\tfrac{1}{2})\pi/N_\theta$ (uniform in $\theta$),
  with weights given by the Kostelec--Rockmore expression
  $w_j^{\rm DH} = (2/N_\theta) \sin\theta_j \sum_{r=0}^{N_\theta/2-1}
   \tfrac{1}{2r+1} \sin\bigl((2r+1)\theta_j\bigr)$~\cite{kostelec_rockmore},
  and $N_\theta = 2(\ell_{\max}+1)$.
\item \emph{Gauss--Legendre:} $\theta_j^{\rm GL}$ are the roots of the
  Legendre polynomial $P_{N_\theta}(\cos\theta_j^{\rm GL}) = 0$
  on $(0, \pi)$, with companion weights
  $w_j^{\rm GL} = 2/\bigl[(1-\cos^2\theta_j)\,(P_{N_\theta}'(\cos\theta_j))^2\bigr]$,
  and $N_\theta = \ell_{\max}+1$ suffices for exactness on
  band-limited products of degree $2\ell_{\max}$.
\end{itemize}
Both yield a flat list of $N = N_\theta N_\phi$ nodes
$(\theta_i, \phi_i)$ with weights $w_i = w_j^{(\theta)} w_k^{(\phi)}$.
We assemble the buffers as
$\texttt{to\_grid\_mat}_{i, (\ell m)} = Y_{\ell m}(\theta_i, \phi_i)$
and
$\texttt{from\_grid\_mat}_{i, (\ell m)} = w_i \, Y_{\ell m}(\theta_i, \phi_i)$,
using e3nn's real spherical harmonics
\texttt{o3.spherical\_harmonics(...,
normalization=\textquotesingle integral\textquotesingle)} and applying the same
$m_{\max}$-cropping and rescaling as fairchem's \texttt{SO3\_Grid}
(\texttt{so3.py:537--575}). Reshaping into the
$[N_\theta, N_\phi, n_{\rm coeffs}]$ layout that
\texttt{S2Activation.forward} expects is a no-op for the
tensor-product cases.

For Lebedev rules the grid does not factorise. The nodes
$\{(\hat r_i, w_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ are pulled from
\texttt{scipy.integrate.lebedev\_rule(degree)}, which returns the
$N$ Cartesian points $\hat r_i \in S^2$ and weights $w_i$ for one of
33 standard rules (degrees $3, 5, 7, \ldots, 131$ with point counts
$6, 14, 26, \ldots, 5810$). To fit EquiformerV2's
$[N_\theta, N_\phi, n_{\rm coeffs}]$ einsum signature without
touching the rest of the network, we reshape the flat $N$-point grid
as $N_\theta = 1$, $N_\phi = N$. The resulting buffer is a
single-axis grid that \texttt{einsum} happily contracts but with less
efficient memory access than a flattened-axis kernel would give; we
discuss this limitation in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Choosing $N_\theta$, $N_\phi$, and Lebedev degree}

The minimum quadrature density at which $S^2$ activation products are
integrated exactly is set by the band limit $\ell_{\max}$. For
products of two functions band-limited to degree $\ell_{\max}$ the
integrand has degree $\leq 2\ell_{\max}$, so:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item DH minimum: $N_\theta = 2(\ell_{\max}+1)$, $N_\phi = 2\ell_{\max}+1$;
\item GL minimum: $N_\theta = \ell_{\max}+1$, $N_\phi = 2\ell_{\max}+1$;
\item Lebedev minimum: smallest available rule of degree
  $\geq 2\ell_{\max}$ (e.g.\ degree 13 = 74 points for
  $\ell_{\max}=6$; degree 21 = 170 points provides comfortable
  headroom).
\end{itemize}
For the EquiformerV2 evaluation in this paper we standardise on
$\ell_{\max} = 6$, $m_{\max} = 2$, where DH default is $14 \times 5
= 70$ points (e3nn's choice) and DH $2\times$ is $28 \times 28 = 784$
points (fairchem's commonly used oversampling override). We compare
these against GL min ($7 \times 13 = 91$ pts), GL match-DH
($14 \times 13 = 182$ pts), GL $2\times$ ($14 \times 28 = 392$ pts),
Lebedev d=13 (74 pts), and Lebedev d=21 (170 pts). All six configurations
are produced by a single call to \texttt{patch\_so3\_grid(model,
method=\ldots, n\_beta=\ldots, n\_alpha=\ldots, lebedev\_degree=\ldots)};
no other code in the model changes.

%========================================================================
\section{Headline: Matched-Equivariance Pareto}
\label{sec:headline}
%========================================================================

\subsection{Setup}
We evaluate on the constructor-default \texttt{EquiformerV2} backbone
in fairchem (12 layers, 128 sphere channels,
$\ell_{\max}{=}6$, $m_{\max}{=}2$; $\sim$30M backbone params,
24~\texttt{S2Activation} modules total). Inputs are QM9 batches of
$8$ molecules; the wall-clock benchmark is architecture-level
(random-init), since the per-forward cost depends only on tensor
shapes and the precomputed grid matrices, not on weight values.
Equivariance error is measured at the kernel level: a single
$\mathcal{A}_{\rm SiLU}$ forward on random SH coefficients
($\ell_{\max}{=}6$, $m_{\max}{=}2$), averaged over 5 random rotations
$\times$ 10 random inputs.

\subsection{Wall-clock protocol}
We run $n=5$ \emph{independent} replicates per configuration. Each
replicate uses a fresh model construction, a fresh random seed for
batch sampling, and 30 measured forwards on \emph{distinct}
QM9 batches drawn without replacement (no batch reuse within or
across replicates). Each forward is wrapped in
\texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()} on both sides; we discard 10
warmup forwards per replicate. We report 95\% confidence intervals
$t_{n-1, 0.975} \times \mathrm{s.e.}(\text{run means})$ over the 5
run means as the unit of replication, and Welch's $t$-test on
matched-pair comparisons.

\subsection{Result}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the Pareto frontier. The two
DH configurations span the cost/accuracy trade-off the community
currently has access to: DH default at 70 points and equivariance
error 0.443, or DH $2\times$ at 784 points and equivariance error
0.327. GL match-DH (182 points) reaches the same equivariance
($0.328$) as DH $2\times$ at the same wall-clock as DH default
($113.17 \pm 2.25$~ms vs.\ $113.50 \pm 1.87$~ms; $\Delta = -0.33 \pm
2.49$~ms, $p = 0.76$, n.s.). Equivalently: switching DH $2\times \to$
GL match-DH saves \textbf{31.3\% wall-clock per forward}
($-51.5 \pm 2.36$~ms, $p < 10^{-4}$) at matched equivariance.

The kernel-level Pareto extends further: a Lebedev rule of degree~21
(170 points, non-tensor-product) reaches the same equivariance floor
at an additional $\sim$28\% lower kernel time
(Appendix~\ref{app:fft}). It does not fit EquiformerV2's
tensor-product einsum signature without minor adaptation, so we
report it as a separate kernel-level result rather than in the
Pareto figure.

\subsection{Where the savings come from}
Hooking CUDA timing events into every \texttt{S2Activation.forward} in
the model (Table~\ref{tab:breakdown}) shows that the $S^2$ activation
kernel is $8.5\%$ of forward time at DH default but balloons to
$39.1\%$ under DH $2\times$. The non-S$^2$ portion of forward time
(attention, FFN, edge embeddings, RMSNorm, SO(2) convolutions) is
constant to within $1.5\%$ across all four configurations. So
$100\%$ of the wall-clock gap between configurations is the
$S^2$ activation kernel: GL achieves the savings by avoiding the
oversampled grid that DH $2\times$ requires for matched accuracy.

\section{Drop-in Safety and the Per-Layer Mechanism}
\label{sec:dropin}
%========================================================================

The Pareto result of \S\ref{sec:headline} is at the
\emph{kernel level} and on a randomly initialised model. We now ask
two follow-up questions. \textbf{(Q1)~Does swapping the grid in an
actual trained checkpoint preserve predictions?}---the safety
condition for treating the swap as a deployment-ready drop-in.
\textbf{(Q2)~If yes, what mechanism explains the safety, and what
does the same mechanism say about how to extract more equivariance
gain?} Both have a clean answer through per-layer measurements of
the $\ell{=}0$ invariance error, which we develop together in this
section.

\subsection{Q1: Inference-time swap preserves predictions}

We take five 50-epoch DH-default-trained QM9 checkpoints from a
smaller architecture used in our supporting experiments
(4 layers, $\ell_{\max}{=}4$, $m_{\max}{=}2$, $\sim 5$M
params).\footnote{We use the smaller architecture because we do not
have a publicly available 12-layer fairchem-default trained checkpoint
for QM9; the architecture-level wall-clock argument of
\S\ref{sec:headline} is weight-independent so the model-size
mismatch does not invalidate it. See \S\ref{sec:limitations}.}
For each of four inference-time grids (DH default, GL min,
GL match-DH, GL $2\times$) we re-evaluate test MAE and prediction
invariance error on the held-out test set, leaving training-time
weights fixed.

Test MAE shifts by at most $0.01$~eV across all four grids
(seed-to-seed training variance is $\sim 0.5$~eV); prediction-
invariance error ratios are $1.00 \pm 0.02$, statistically
indistinguishable from unity. The drop-in is safe.

\subsection{Q2: Per-layer profile preservation explains why}

We instrument each transformer block with a forward hook capturing
the $\ell{=}0$ component (an SO(3)-invariant scalar field) at the
block's output, and report
$\|e_i^{\ell{=}0} - e_i^{\ell{=}0,(R)}\|_F /
   \|e_i^{\ell{=}0}\|_F$ averaged over 10 rotations and 20 batches.
Table~\ref{tab:dropin_perlayer} shows that for these
DH-default-trained checkpoints the per-layer profile is identical
across grids at the percent level: $L_0 \approx 5.3 \times 10^{-5}$,
the same $L_0 \to L_1$ jump ratio of $\sim$$9.4\times$, the same
plateau. The trained model has internalised its training-time grid;
swapping the grid post-hoc moves nothing.

\subsection{The two-phase pattern is universal; only the floor
varies with training}

Across all activations and grids tested
(Table~\ref{tab:perlayer}), the $\ell{=}0$ error follows the same
two-phase shape: a sharp jump $L_0 \to L_1$ ($1.5$--$15.7\times$
depending on activation smoothness), then a plateau through layers
$2$--$3$. What \emph{does} differ between trained checkpoints is the
\emph{absolute floor}, and---perhaps surprisingly---the dominant
control over the floor is the \emph{number of grid points}, not the
quadrature method. Training SiLU with GL match-DH (182 points,

\subsection{Mechanism: a $\delta$/$c$ toy model}

Let $\delta$ be the typical per-S$^2$-Act perturbation of an
$\ell{>}0$ component, and let $c \ll 1$ be the cross-degree coupling
factor by which $\ell{>}0$ corruption leaks into $\ell{=}0$ (via
RMSNorm's all-degree normalisation and the SO(2) convolution's
$m{=}0$ \texttt{nn.Linear} that mixes $\ell{=}0$ with $\ell{>}0$
coefficients).
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Layer 0 input:} $\ell{=}0$ error is exactly 0 (\texttt{sphere\_embedding} is rotation-invariant by construction); $\ell{>}0$ error is exactly 0 (\texttt{EdgeDegreeEmbedding} is rotation-equivariant by construction).
\item \textbf{Layer 0 output:} EquiformerV2's
  \texttt{SeparableS2Activation} routes $\ell{=}0$ through a scalar
  SiLU that does not see the S$^2$ grid, so layer~0 has only
  \emph{indirect} $\ell{=}0$ contamination,
  $L_0 \sim c \cdot \delta$. The $\ell{>}0$ component sees one
  $S^2$-Act forward, so it picks up the full $\delta$.
\item \textbf{Layer 1 output:} The input now carries $\ell{>}0$
  corruption $\sim \delta$. Attention aggregates corrupted neighbour
  features through learned linear projections and produces new
  $\ell{=}0$ values directly from the corrupted $\ell{>}0$ input
  (a \emph{first-order} path). So $L_1 \sim O(1) \cdot \delta$, and
  $L_1/L_0 \sim 1/c$, the inverse of the cross-degree coupling
  factor.
\end{itemize}
This explains both the empirical jump ratio and its activation
dependence: smooth activations have small $c$ (their $\ell{=}0$
channels are mostly invariant, and RMSNorm fluctuations from
$\ell{>}0$ are second-order), so the jump is large
($8$--$16\times$); non-smooth activations have large $\delta$,
indirect leak is no longer perturbative, and the ratio collapses to
$1.5$--$1.9\times$. After layer~1 both $\ell{=}0$ and $\ell{>}0$
carry comparable errors; residual connections plus RMSNorm bound
further growth and the relative error saturates.

\subsection{Why infinite-grid-at-inference would not help}

A natural objection: in the limit of an infinitely dense grid the
$S^2$-Activation operator becomes exact, so the trained-model
invariance error \emph{should} approach zero. Why doesn't a denser
inference grid visibly reduce the per-layer error in
Table~\ref{tab:dropin_perlayer}? Two facts together resolve this.
(i)~The kernel-level equivariance error at $\ell_{\max}{=}6$,
$m_{\max}{=}2$ saturates at $\sim 0.327$ regardless of grid; the
floor comes from $m_{\max}{=}2$ truncation, which discards $|m| > 2$
components and is not a quadrature error any grid can fix.
(ii)~Trained features concentrate in the part of the basis
$m_{\max}{=}2$ \emph{keeps} (low-$m$, $\ell{=}0$-dominated), so the
truncation floor barely hits them. Their residual error is bounded
above by $\|c\| \cdot \|\ell{>}0\text{ features}\|$, and the second
factor is small \emph{because training drove it small} against the
training-time grid. Inference-time grid swap perturbs the
$\ell{>}0$ features by $\sim 1\%$; the corresponding $\ell{=}0$
leak is the same percentage of an already-small number, hence the
percent-level invariance of Table~\ref{tab:dropin_perlayer}. Going
to an infinitely dense grid at inference cannot drive the floor
lower because there is no gradient telling the trained weights to
make the $\ell{>}0$ residual any smaller: that optimization happens
only at training time.

\subsection{Practical implications}

The inference-time drop-in is therefore a \emph{free wall-clock
reduction at preserved task accuracy}, not a way to tighten
trained-model equivariance. The harder question---how to actually
lower the trained-model invariance floor---has a more nuanced answer
than ``train with the better quadrature.''
Empirically (Table~\ref{tab:perlayer}, $n=5$ converged seeds), GL
match-DH at training time gives a per-layer profile statistically
indistinguishable from DH default; the GL/DH method choice does
\emph{not} by itself shift the trained-model floor. The intervention
that does shift the floor is increasing the \emph{grid-point count}
substantially, as DH~$2\times$ illustrates with its $11\times$ point
density. The natural prescription combining both observations is to
train with a denser GL grid (e.g.~GL~$2\times$ at 392 points), which
matches DH~$2\times$ on kernel-level equivariance at lower wall-clock
than DH~$2\times$ (\S\ref{sec:headline}); we leave this combined
training run for future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%========================================================================

\paragraph{The S$^2$-activation lineage.}
SCN~\cite{scn} introduced the S$^2$-activation operator for
SO(3)-equivariant atomistic networks; eSCN~\cite{escn} reduced its
SO(3) convolutions to SO(2) for efficiency; EquiformerV2~\cite
{equiformerv2} integrated this into a transformer backbone and
explicitly noted that ``sampling-induced equivariance error remains
an open challenge.'' All three inherit e3nn's equiangular DH grid
without modification.

\paragraph{Concurrent work, complementary levers.}
\emph{EquiformerV3}~\cite{equiformerv3} (April 2026, by the V2
authors) addresses the same equivariance problem at the
\emph{architectural} level: SwiGLU-S$^2$ replaces the S$^2$ activation
with a SwiGLU operating only on scalars and bilinear products,
avoiding the grid round-trip entirely and achieving a $50.6\%$
reduction in grid points while maintaining strict equivariance. It
requires retraining and a new model.
\emph{EST}~\cite{est} (May 2025) addresses it at the
\emph{sampling-pattern} level: replaces the e3nn grid with a
Fibonacci lattice, observing that e3nn's polar clustering wastes
points. It also requires retraining.
Our work targets a third, complementary lever---the \emph{quadrature
rule}---and operates as a drop-in change for both pretrained
checkpoints and from-scratch training.

\paragraph{Community awareness of the problem.}
The fairchem issue tracker contains \texttt{issue \#1068} (March
2025)~\cite{fairchem1068}, where users reported the equivariance
problem and discussed mitigations including ``more grid points''
and double precision, both prohibitively expensive. None of the
proposed fixes considered alternative quadrature rules.

%========================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%========================================================================

\textbf{$m_{\max}$ truncation is a separate floor.} The kernel-level
equivariance saturates at $\sim 0.327$ at $\ell_{\max}{=}6$,
$m_{\max}{=}2$ regardless of how dense the grid is. This is a
truncation error from the eSCN reduction, not a quadrature error;
no choice of quadrature can break it. Models that use
$m_{\max}{=}\ell_{\max}$ (no truncation) reach a much lower floor
($\sim 4 \times 10^{-3}$ at $\ell_{\max}{=}6$) where the GL advantage
is even more pronounced.

\textbf{No fully-trained 12-layer GL run yet.} Our trained-checkpoint
experiments use the smaller 4-layer expF architecture because we do
not have a publicly available 12-layer fairchem-default QM9 checkpoint.
The architecture-level wall-clock claim is weight-independent, so it
transfers, but extending the per-layer floor data
(Table~\ref{tab:perlayer}) to 12 layers is left to future work.

\textbf{Lebedev does not fit the tensor-product einsum natively.}
Lebedev d=21 is the Pareto-optimal kernel-level choice in our
benchmark (Appendix~\ref{app:fft}), but its non-tensor-product
structure requires reshape-as-$1{\times}N$ within EquiformerV2's
einsum, which is suboptimal. Native support would require a small
refactor of \texttt{S2Activation.forward}.

\textbf{Public-checkpoint loading hit module-shape mismatches.} We
attempted to load the fairchem OC20 EquiformerV2-31M public
checkpoint into our QM9 model wrapper; mismatches in the
\texttt{rad\_func.net} radial-function widths
(\texttt{share\_atom\_edge\_embedding}, etc.) prevented exact
loading. The architecture-level wall-clock for that configuration is
in Appendix~\ref{app:scale}; the drop-in safety result remains
established only on our own trained checkpoints.

%========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%========================================================================

We have argued that the choice of quadrature method in EquiformerV2's
$S^2$ activation is a useful and underexplored lever: orthogonal to
the architectural changes of EquiformerV3 and the sampling-pattern
changes of EST. We instantiated two alternatives---Gauss--Legendre
and Lebedev---in a drop-in \texttt{CustomSO3Grid} module, and showed
that switching to GL match-DH delivers DH $2\times$'s equivariance
floor at $31.3\%$ lower wall-clock per forward, with no retraining
and no measurable change in trained-model predictions. Per-layer
measurements expose a universal two-phase mechanism that explains
both the safety of the inference-time swap and the complementary
recommendation to train with a finer grid when one wants to lower
trained-model invariance error---both consistent with our
$\delta/c$ toy model.

\paragraph{Future work.}
(i)~A 50-epoch GL match-DH training sweep on the 12-layer fairchem
default to close the trained-model invariance gap on the headline
architecture. (ii)~A native Lebedev path through EquiformerV2's
$S^2$-Act forward, eliminating the $1\!\times\!N$ reshape and
allowing the kernel-Pareto-best Lebedev d=21 to deploy without
adaptation. (iii)~A unified architectural+quadrature ablation
combining EquiformerV3's SwiGLU-S$^2$ with GL grids, since the two
levers act on different parts of the activation pipeline and are
expected to compound.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Exact integration $\Rightarrow$ exact equivariance}{Exact integration implies exact equivariance}}
\label{app:exact-equivariance}

We formalise the claim from \S\ref{sec:bg} that the equivariance error
of $\mathcal{A}_\sigma$ vanishes when the quadrature in
\eqref{eq:s2act} is replaced by the exact integral. Define the
\emph{continuous} $S^2$-activation operator
$\mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma$ acting on bandlimited
$f \in V_{\ell_{\max}} := \mathrm{span}\{Y_{\ell m} : 0 \leq \ell
\leq \ell_{\max}\}$ by
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma f\bigr)(\theta, \phi)
  \;=\;
  \sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\max}} \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}
  \biggl(\,
    \int_{S^2} \sigma\!\bigl(f(\theta', \phi')\bigr)\,
                 Y_{\ell m}(\theta', \phi')\, d\Omega'
  \biggr)\,
  Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi),
  \label{eq:exact-op}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ apply $\sigma$ pointwise on $S^2$ and project back onto
$V_{\ell_{\max}}$ via exact integration. In SH-coefficient
coordinates this is exactly \eqref{eq:s2act} with the quadrature sum
$\sum_i w_i (\cdot)$ replaced by the integral
$\int_{S^2} (\cdot)\,d\Omega$.

Let $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ act on functions on $S^2$ by
$(R \cdot f)(\theta,\phi) = f(R^{-1} \cdot (\theta,\phi))$, and let
$\mathcal{D}(R)$ denote the Wigner-$D$ representation acting on the
SH-coefficient vector. We show:

\begin{proposition}
For every measurable $\sigma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that
$\sigma \circ f \in L^2(S^2)$ for all $f \in V_{\ell_{\max}}$ and
every $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma(\mathcal{D}(R)\,c)
  \;=\;
  \mathcal{D}(R)\,\mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma(c)
  \qquad \text{for all } c \in \mathbb{R}^{(\ell_{\max}+1)^2}.
  \label{eq:exact-equivariance}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Two ingredients suffice: (i) the action of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ on
$V_{\ell_{\max}}$ via the Wigner matrices is the same as the rotation
of the underlying function on the sphere, and (ii) the Haar measure
$d\Omega$ on $S^2$ is rotation-invariant.

Concretely, let $f(\theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell, m} c_{\ell m}\,Y_{\ell
m}(\theta, \phi)$ be the band-limited function with coefficients $c$.
By construction of the Wigner-$D$ representation,
\begin{equation}
  R \cdot f
  \;=\;
  \sum_{\ell, m} \bigl[\mathcal{D}(R)\,c\bigr]_{\ell m}\,
  Y_{\ell m},
  \label{eq:wigner-action}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ rotating the function and rotating its coefficient vector are
the same operation. Now compute the $(\ell, m)$-coefficient of
$\mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma$ applied to $\mathcal{D}(R)\,c$:
\begin{align}
  \bigl[\mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma(\mathcal{D}(R)\,c)\bigr]_{\ell m}
  &\;=\; \int_{S^2} \sigma\!\bigl((R \cdot f)(\theta, \phi)\bigr)\,
         Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)\,d\Omega
   \tag{by \eqref{eq:exact-op}, \eqref{eq:wigner-action}} \\
  &\;=\; \int_{S^2} \sigma\!\bigl(f(R^{-1} \cdot (\theta, \phi))\bigr)\,
         Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)\,d\Omega
   \tag{def.\ of $R \cdot f$} \\
  &\;=\; \int_{S^2} \sigma\!\bigl(f(\theta', \phi')\bigr)\,
         Y_{\ell m}(R \cdot (\theta', \phi'))\,d\Omega'
   \tag{$d\Omega = d\Omega'$, $\Omega' = R^{-1}\Omega$} \\
  &\;=\; \int_{S^2} \sigma\!\bigl(f(\theta', \phi')\bigr)\,
         \Bigl(\sum_{m'} \mathcal{D}(R)_{(\ell m), (\ell m')}\,
                          Y_{\ell m'}(\theta', \phi')\Bigr)\,d\Omega'
   \tag{$Y_{\ell m}$ are an SO(3)-irrep} \\
  &\;=\; \sum_{m'} \mathcal{D}(R)_{(\ell m), (\ell m')}\,
         \int_{S^2} \sigma\!\bigl(f(\theta', \phi')\bigr)\,
                      Y_{\ell m'}(\theta', \phi')\,d\Omega' \\
  &\;=\; \sum_{m'} \mathcal{D}(R)_{(\ell m), (\ell m')}\,
         \bigl[\mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma(c)\bigr]_{\ell m'}
   \tag{by \eqref{eq:exact-op}} \\
  &\;=\; \bigl[\mathcal{D}(R)\,
         \mathcal{A}^{\star}_\sigma(c)\bigr]_{\ell m}.
\end{align}
The third equality is the rotation invariance of the Haar measure on
$S^2$; the fourth uses that each subspace
$V_\ell = \mathrm{span}\{Y_{\ell m}\}_m$ is an SO(3)-irrep on which
$\mathcal{D}(R)$ acts blockwise. Equation \eqref{eq:exact-equivariance}
follows.
\end{proof}

Two remarks. First, the result holds for \emph{any} pointwise $\sigma$
under mild integrability assumptions; nothing in the proof uses
smoothness or band-limited-ness of $\sigma(f)$. Second, the proof
identifies precisely where the discrete operator
\eqref{eq:s2act} departs from \eqref{eq:exact-op}: replacing
$\int_{S^2} g(\theta', \phi')\,d\Omega'$ by $\sum_i w_i\,
g(\theta_i, \phi_i)$ uses the rotation invariance of $d\Omega$ via a
change of variables $\Omega' = R^{-1}\Omega$, which has no
finite-sum analogue when $\{(\theta_i, \phi_i)\}$ is not a fixed
point set of every $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$. Equivalently, a finite
quadrature is exact only on a finite-dimensional polynomial subspace
of $L^2(S^2)$, and $\sigma(f)$ generically leaves that subspace
(Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}, bottom); the residual
high-$\ell$ content aliases into the kept coefficients in a
rotation-dependent way.

\section{Scaling savings across architectures}
\label{app:scale}

The $31\%$ saving in the headline is for the 12-layer fairchem-default
architecture. Two other published configurations show how the saving
scales with depth and band limit
(Figure~\ref{fig:sweep}): \textbf{QM9-small} (4 layers / 64 channels /
$\ell_{\max}{=}4$, our trained-checkpoint testbed) shows
$+15.4\%$; \textbf{OC20 EquiformerV2-31M} (8 layers / 128 channels /
$\ell_{\max}{=}4$, the public OC20 checkpoint we attempted to load)
shows $+13.9\%$; the \textbf{fairchem-default 12-layer} model shows
$+31.2\%$. The pattern is monotonic in $\ell_{\max}$: at
$\ell_{\max}{=}4$ the DH $2\times$ grid is $20\!\times\!20 = 400$
points (a $5.7$--$8\times$ increase over default), while at
$\ell_{\max}{=}6$ DH $2\times$ is $28\!\times\!28 = 784$ points

from the swap because the $S^2$-activation kernel becomes a larger

\section{Why FFT does not save DH at this scale}
\label{app:fft}

A natural objection to swapping DH for GL is that the Driscoll--Healy
grid was designed precisely to enable an FFT-based fast SH
transform---an algorithmic advantage GL forfeits, since GL nodes do
not admit a fast algorithm. This appendix shows that the advantage
is theoretical only at EquiformerV2's scale, and that even when
explicitly wired in is slower than dense matmul.

DH's classical justification is its FFT-based fast SH transform: at
$\ell_{\max} \gtrsim 64$ this gives a decisive
$O(\ell_{\max}^2 \log^2 \ell_{\max})$ vs.\ the dense
$O(\ell_{\max}^4)$. EquiformerV2 operates at
$\ell_{\max} \in \{4, 6, 8\}$ where this asymptotic advantage has not
yet emerged on a GPU; and as we discuss below, its
\texttt{SO3\_Grid} precomputes the SH projection as a single dense
matrix and never invokes the FFT path that e3nn does provide for
some configurations. Once we are in the dense-matmul regime, DH's
design advantage is unrealised, and the question becomes purely
numerical: which nodes integrate band-limited products with the
fewest points. The answer points to GL and Lebedev, as
\S\ref{sec:headline} makes precise.

The remainder of this appendix gives three observations explaining
why DH's FFT advantage is not realised in EquiformerV2.

\paragraph{(i) e3nn implements only the longitude FFT.}
e3nn's \texttt{ToS2Grid.forward} (\texttt{e3nn/o3/\_s2grid.py:367})
takes the FFT path along longitude only when $N_\alpha$ is odd and
$\geq 2\ell_{\max}+1$; the Legendre direction is always a dense
matmul. The full Driscoll--Healy fast Legendre transform is not
implemented in e3nn (and in practice has a high break-even
$\ell_{\max} \gtrsim 64$ on GPU).

\paragraph{(ii) EquiformerV2 bypasses the FFT entirely.}
\texttt{SO3\_Grid} (lines 529--578 of fairchem's \texttt{so3.py})
calls \texttt{ToS2Grid} only at construction time, collapses
\texttt{shb} and \texttt{sha} into a single dense
\texttt{to\_grid\_mat} of shape $[N_\beta, N_\alpha, n_{\rm coeffs}]$,
and applies it at runtime via a single \texttt{einsum}. The FFT
branch in \texttt{ToS2Grid.forward} is never reached.

\paragraph{(iii) Wiring up the FFT path makes the kernel slower.}
We re-implemented \texttt{S2Activation} so that it explicitly invokes
\texttt{ToS2Grid.forward} / \texttt{FromS2Grid.forward} at runtime
and benchmarked against the dense path at the same grid size. At a
$14 \times 13 = 182$-point grid, the FFT path is \emph{$7\times$
slower} than dense matmul (Table~\ref{tab:four_way}). The
$m_{\max}$-cropping inflate/scatter step plus a much larger
\texttt{shb} einsum (over the full $(\ell_{\max}+1)^2 = 49$ basis
instead of the cropped 29 used by the dense path) wipes out the
savings of the alpha FFT at $N_\alpha = 13$.

The same table shows that Lebedev at degree~21 (170 points,
non-tensor-product) is the kernel-level Pareto winner: same
equivariance floor as DH $2\times$ and GL match-DH, at 28\% lower
kernel time than GL match-DH and 33\% lower than DH-dense match.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

All experiments run on Perlmutter under the
\texttt{module load pytorch/2.6.0-1} environment (Python 3.12.12,
PyTorch 2.6.0, CUDA 12.4, e3nn 0.4.4, fairchem-core 1.10.0,
torch\_geometric 2.7.0). Two non-obvious gotchas: PyTorch 2.6's
\texttt{weights\_only=True} default breaks e3nn's
\texttt{constants.pt}, requiring
\texttt{torch.serialization.add\_safe\_globals([slice])} before any
e3nn import; and the 12-layer / lmax=6 model fragments the CUDA
allocator unless \texttt{PYTORCH\_CUDA\_ALLOC\_CONF=expandable\_segments:True}.

The exact commands to reproduce the four benchmarks of this paper
are:
\begin{verbatim}
# Headline Pareto (Section 4)
PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True \
  python3 experiments/expG_verify_speedup_v2.py \
    --batch_size 8 --n_runs 5 --n_iter 30 --n_warmup 10
# Drop-in safety on trained checkpoints (Section 5.1-5.2)
PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True \
  python3 experiments/expG_dropin_pretrained.py \
    --seeds 123 456 789 1024 --activation SiLU --orig_grid default
# Architecture sweep (Appendix A)
PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True \
  python3 experiments/expG_sweep_models.py
# Four-way kernel benchmark (Appendix B)
PYTORCH_CUDA_ALLOC_CONF=expandable_segments:True \
  python3 experiments/expG_four_way.py
\end{verbatim}
